# Editorial standards

## One Short, one idea

Every package answers exactly one central question. The narration should make a
single evidence-backed point, complicate the obvious assumption, and leave the
viewer with a useful way to see the animal. Lists, tangential facts, and
unrelated trivia belong in another package.

The required shape is:

1. **Hook (1–3 seconds).** A concrete tension or surprising claim, not a generic
   greeting. It must also be the first scene's narration.
2. **Grounding.** State what was observed or tested, including the relevant
   sample or context when the source supports it.
3. **Evidence.** Explain the finding without converting correlation into proof of
   an inner state.
4. **Limit.** Name what the study cannot show.
5. **Reinforcing ending.** Return to the question in plain language and give a
   practical takeaway. The ending must not introduce a new claim.

The first batch targets 45 seconds and 4–8 scenes, with six scenes per package.
The channel configuration permits 30–60 seconds.

## Language

- Use warm, measured, natural English. Read aloud before approving.
- Prefer short sentences and concrete nouns. Do not stack clauses to hit a clock.
- Say "the study found" or "the authors suggest" when that is what happened.
- Keep the narration within the configured 1.7–3.3 words-per-second range for
  scenes at least four seconds long. Shorter hook scenes have a slightly wider
  tolerance to make a crisp opening possible.
- Avoid unsupported certainty: "always," "never fails," "guaranteed," "miracle,"
  "scientists say," "studies prove," and "experts agree" are forbidden.
- Avoid veterinary advice language such as "cure," "medication," "dose,"
  "prescribe," "diagnose," and "treat your pet." The channel is educational, not
  clinical.
- Do not use a quote unless it has been checked against the source. Do not invent
  numbers, study details, authors, journals, or links.

## Evidence language

Use the narrowest sentence the source can support:

- **Supported:** "The study recorded fourteen dogs."
- **Partially supported:** "The pattern is consistent with attention to owner cues."
- **Interpretation:** "That makes the look a poor guilt detector."
- **Unknown:** "The experiment did not test what the dogs felt."

Anecdotes can illustrate a question but cannot establish a scientific fact. A
news article can provide context or a named real-life story; it is not sufficient
for a biological claim on its own.

## Visual direction

Visuals are illustrative, not archival. Each scene needs:

- a specific subject with consistent coat, markings, eyes, and proportions;
- a shot type, camera motion, vertical framing, and continuity note;
- at least two avoid notes;
- a welfare-safe action. Calm uncertainty is acceptable; staged fear, pain,
  injury, aggression, punishment, or distress is not;
- no request for text, logos, watermarks, or human faces unless the story
  explicitly requires them and the handling is respectful.

Never ask a generator to depict a real named animal as if the output were
authentic footage. Never use generated images as evidence. Reject malformed
anatomy: extra limbs, tails, ears, eyes, merged hands, or impossible joints.

Every package and the final description must carry the exact disclosure:

> Illustrative, AI-assisted visuals; not documentary footage.

## Titles, descriptions, and tags

- Titles must be accurate, under 100 characters, and free of unsupported
  certainty. Curiosity is fine; deception is not.
- The description states the central fact, the takeaway, a `Sources:` section,
  the synthetic-media disclosure, the educational disclaimer, and `#Shorts`.
- Hashtags and tags describe the actual topic; they do not imply a breed,
  diagnosis, or guarantee.
- Visibility defaults to private. `made_for_kids` is false for this channel.
- Do not use a thumbnail or title that manufactures the emotion the video is
  questioning. For example, do not pair a "guilty dog" claim with a staged
  cowering image.

## Human review

Automated QA can establish that fields exist and policies match. It cannot judge
whether a cut feels manipulative, whether a purr recording sounds urgent, or
whether a generated dog has an uncanny ear. A human editor must watch the final
render end to end, verify citations, and decide whether the Short is fit to
publish. No upload integration exists by design.
